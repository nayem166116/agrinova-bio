Soil microbiome resilience is a rotation decision, not a product decision

11 min read · Soil science · 4 February 2026

There is a version of the biological-input pitch that treats soil as an empty container waiting to be filled with better organisms. It sells well and it is wrong. A hectare of arable topsoil already contains several tonnes of living biomass. Adding a formulated inoculant to that is not filling a container; it is introducing a small number of specialists into a crowded, competitive system.

Persistence is the honest metric

Our long-term sites track applied strains by strain-specific qPCR across seasons. In continuously cropped cereal fields with intensive tillage, applied Bacillus strains are typically undetectable by the following spring. In the same soil type under a diversified rotation with a cover crop and reduced disturbance, detection persisted into the second season at 14 of 38 sites. The product was identical. The system around it was not.

Three levers that outrank any inoculant

  • Living roots for more of the year. Continuous root exudation is the primary carbon input for the soil microbial community. A cover crop is not a marketing gesture; it is the food supply.
  • Less disturbance. Mycorrhizal hyphal networks are physically destroyed by inversion tillage. There is no formulation that survives being ploughed into fragments.
  • Residue returned in a usable state. Standing dry stubble is slow carbon. Moist, partially broken-down residue is available carbon, which is the reason a residue product exists in our line at all.

Where inoculants still earn their place

None of this argues against inoculation. It argues for sequencing. On a field that has just come out of a long tillage-heavy cereal run, a mycorrhizal product applied on its own is close to a donation. The same product applied in the second year of a diversified rotation, into soil with living roots and undisturbed structure, has somewhere to establish.

Inoculants are best understood as an accelerant. They speed up a transition that the rotation has already made possible.

A practical test

Before ordering, ask whether anything about the system has changed in the last three seasons. If the answer is no — same rotation, same tillage, same residue handling — then a biological input is being asked to do a job the system will undo. Change one structural thing first. Then inoculate, and measure it against a strip that had the structural change but no product.
